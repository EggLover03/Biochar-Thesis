\section{Importance of mint as a medicinal and spice plant}

\subsection{Mint (\textit{Mentha spp.})}

The species of mint have an economic importance, and are used in different industrial sectors (food, pharmaceutical and cosmetic) for their medicinal and aromatic properties \parencite{Zheljazkov2020}. Among these, \textit{Mentha piperita}, \textit{M. spicata} and \textit{M. crispata} are of particular interest for the capacity of essential oil production and biomass production.

Mint has been a medicinal herb since the olden days. Mint is used in the historical documents of Nicander of Colophon \textcite{Silva2020} to make a snake repellant using a stench. It indicates that mint is used in traditional medicine and plays a major role in therapeutic applications.

Mint is also appreciated for its composition of essential oils. The essential oils from peppermint leave are present in 1.2-3.9\% of the total peppermint leaves and over 300 compounds have been identified. Of these, the terpenic class is by far the most abundant with about 52\% monoterpenes, and 9\% sesquiterpenes, with other classes such as aldehyde (9\%), alcohol (6\%), miscellaneous compounds (8\%), lactone (7\%) and aromatic hydrocarbons (9\%) in lower proportions \textcite{Silva2020}. Botanically, mint (\textit{Mentha spp.}) is an aromatic herb, mostly perennial. It is a well known medicinal and edible herb. The use of \textit{Mentha spp.} has been extended since ancient civilizations and remains pertinent to the fields of medicine and traditional health systems, and also in the food industry \textcite{Silva2020}.

\subsection{Mint as a medicine plant}

Mint is a medicinal herb which has been used for a long time, particularly as a digestive tonic. In previous studies \textit{Mentha} species have been reported to have carminative, anti-spasmodic, anti-emetic, antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and insecticidal properties. The essential oils and phenolic compounds, such as menthol and menthone, flavonoids and phenolic acids, are responsible for its medicinal properties.

Amongst the most researched species is peppermint (\textit{Mentha piperita}) which is used for gastrointestinal ailments such as indigestion, flatulence, colic, nausea and irritable bowel syndrome. The antimicrobial activity exhibited by the mint essential oils against a wide range of bacteria and fungi is a well-informed reason for their traditional applications in food preservation and against infectious diseases. Also the relaxing, cooling and anti-septic action of the mint oils and menthol make them highly useful in pharmaceutical preparations, oral hygiene products, cosmetics and aromatherapy. Its medicinal uses are also emphasized and mint was used medicinally in Ancient Babylonian, Greek, Roman, Islamic, and Medieval European medicine. Given that `Mints have been used since ancient Babylon, but it was in Classical Antiquity that their medical uses blossomed' \parencite{Silva2020} and that the 500 BCE Greek physician Hippocrates used them, it would be a safe bet to say that they have been used for some time as medicine.

\subsection{The importance of mint as a spice plant has increased.}

Mint is also an important spice crop for many cultures, in addition to its medicinal value. Edible parts of the plant: leaves, fresh or dried; in most cases fresh mint is preferred.

Fresh leaves are preferred as they will have a stronger aroma and taste. The leaves of the mint are warm, sweet and refreshing with a unique cooling effect. Thus they are widely applied in different foods and drinks such as sauces, jellos, confectioneries, syrups, ice creams and traditional dishes. Mint is an essential herb in Middle Eastern cuisine, Indian, European and African cuisines, where it is used to improve the taste, and as a digestive aid. Mint EO's are also well applied in food industry as natural flavouring and preservative agents because of their antimicrobial properties.

\subsection{Economic and cultural significance}

Mint is significant as a medicinal plant, as a spice and is an economically important crop in many countries of the world. Production and processing of fresh and dried mint leaves is of utmost importance for agriculture and industry.

Mint remains an important plant today, used in medicine and food, cosmetics and perfumery, and continues to be a versatile and universally important plant. They are still used for many purposes including non-medicinal, hence are of economic importance herbs \parencite{Silva2020}.

\section{Botanical and economic importance of \textit{Mentha piperita}, 
\textit{Mentha spicata}, and \textit{Mentha crispata}}

\textit{Mentha piperita} (peppermint) is an economically important mint in terms of the presence of menthol and menthone. Menthol produces a ``cooling effect'' and is used in drugs, mouth fresheners, cosmetics and as a food flavour. Carvone is the main compound in \textit{Mentha spicata} (spearmint) essential oil.

Spearmint's mild and sweet flavor is valued in perfumery, in the flavoring industry (particularly in chewing gum and toothpaste), in beverages and confectionery. \textit{Mentha crispata} (curly mint) is considered a variety of spearmint, and is characterized by the curled or wrinkled leaves. Commonly used in cooking, salads, herbal teas and garnishes, and also in essential oils.

\section{Peat moss for indoor and greenhouse mint culture.}

Peat moss is widely used as a growing media in greenhouse culture due to its physical characteristics, however, there are many environmental concerns associated with the extraction of peat moss including carbon emissions and peatland degradation \parencite{Cleary2021}.

There are approximately 150 million hectares of drained peat used for horticulture, which stores about a third of the world's soil carbon, but large emitters of CO\textsubscript{2} after drainage. Peatlands account for almost 5\% of the global CO\textsubscript{2} emissions, which is more than the emissions from international aviation and shipping.

Peat harvesting also entails loss of valuable habitat and biodiversity in the sensitive ecosystems of Canada's Hudson Bay Lowlands. The other one is sustainability because peat grows very slowly (about 1mm per year), but removing it is much faster so it is a non-renewable resource. These environmental problems have resulted in regulations like the policy for the use of peat in horticulture to be phased out in the UK by 2030 and the EU's aim to reduce reliance on peat. For this reason, biochar has been proposed as a sustainable growing media ingredient due to its porous structure and the ability to improve growing media properties \parencite{Dumroese2018}. Despite its horticultural advantages, the environmental issues associated with peat moss use further highlight the need for alternative growing media.

With the increasing popularity of a high quality essential oil, it is imperative that the factors that influence plant growth and physiological properties are better understood.

\section{Growth Requirements and Physiology of Mint}

\subsection{\textit{Mentha piperita} (Peppermint)}

\subsection*{Botanical Characteristics}

\textit{Mentha x piperita} is an erect, rhizomatous perennial herbaceous plant.

\textcite{rhs2026mentha} says that peppermint is a hybrid variety of \textit{Mentha aquatica} (watermint) and \textit{Mentha spicata} (spearmint) and is considered a species, but is now known to be a hybrid. It produces tall stems, dark green leaves and reddish stem with warm and spicy scent.

\subsection*{Requirements for climate and environment.}

\textcite{virzo1980adaptability} demonstrated that \textit{mentha piperita} growth in height at 44\% daylight was better than at 100\% daylight.

They also discovered that at 14\% daylight, the growth is limited presumably because of inadequate photosynthate and flowering is absent. They conducted their transfers from light to shade and shade to light, and found peppermint to be extremely sensitive to irradiance in terms of specific leaf area and chlorophyll content at the seedling stage and also at a late stage of life cycle. Environmental factors: extreme temperatures can lower yield and increase disease susceptibility with respect to temperature. The \textcite{umd2026growing} states that in some climates some shade may be necessary in the summer heat as this can stunt plant growth in subtropical areas.

According to the \textcite{mobot2026piperita}, the plant is a medium to wet moisture plant.

\subsection*{Physiological responses to nutrient stress}

It is also investigated how photosynthetic carbon metabolism and essential oil biogenesis are linked in peppermint plants during manganese deficiency. Mn deficiency had significant effect decreasing CO\textsubscript{2} exchange rate, total chlorophyll, total assimilatory area, plant dry weight and essential oil yield and increased significantly the Chl a/b, LAR and leaf stem ratio. However, the incorporation of CO\textsubscript{2} into the primary metabolic pool and essential oil was significantly less in leaves of Mn deficient plants \parencite{srivastava1994relationship}. Peppermint has also been investigated in relation to iron deficiency.

CO\textsubscript{2} exchange rate, stomatal conductance, oil content, the amount and the ratio of chlorophyll a/b significantly affected. Growth parameters such as leaf area ratio, specific leaf weight and leaf stem ratio \parencite{srivastava1993relation} were also affected as a result of the iron stress.

\subsection*{Soil and Nutrient Requirements}

Peppermint will grow well on most soils of average fertility, except dry soil \parencite{umd2026growing}.

Soil quality such as organic matter and available nutrients are important for good growth.

\subsection{\textit{Mentha spicata} (Spearmint)}

\subsection*{Botanical Characteristics}

Spearmint (\textit{Mentha spicata}) has pointed, slightly crinkled foliage which is lighter green than peppermint, and the entire plant has a distinctive sweet aroma, according to the \textcite{rhs2026spicata}. Perennial that spreads by creeping rhizome, aromatic.

\subsection*{The Physiological responses to water stress.}

Field experiment was carried out in Southern Italy with spearmint plants subjected to different irrigation treatments to evaluate the effect of increasing water stress on subsequent newly emerged leaves, plant water status and net photosynthesis, from the onset of water stress. Limitation of photosynthesis had a very strong influence on the harvest period. In the severe water stress condition, the crop decreased water loss by regulating the stomatal closure and reduced mesophyll conductance. Irrigation treatments did not influence the amount of organic compounds, however, the yield of essential oils was negatively influenced by water stress as the yield of the crop and the total biomass, leaf area index and plant height were reduced \parencite{marino2019evaluation}.

\subsection*{In Vitro Culture Responses}

The work of \textcite{tisserat2004influence} regarding effects of physical parameters on spearmint cultures in vitro, revealed that physical support had a significant effect on the morphogenesis and growth of the cultures. They discovered that the liquid medium is suitable for growing mint shootlets, thus achieving a fourfold increase in fresh weight as compared with the agar medium. Positive relationships between growth, morphogenesis and carvone content to culture vessel size were obtained. In an automated plant culture system, 15-fold of biomass was produced as compared to the culture tubes. These findings were confirmed by the more recent studies of \textcite{tisserat2008growth} who revealed that carvone was only produced in cultured spearmint shoots, but not in cultured spearmint roots or callus cultures.

They also found that the growth and morphogenesis and secondary metabolism were significantly affected by the number of media culture immersions; with twelve immersions per day being the optimum for both growth and morphogenesis.

\subsection*{Comparative Growth Studies}

\textcite{yaghobvand2022evaluation} conducted an aeroponic test for 5 mint species under greenhouse conditions. They also discovered that \textit{M. spicata} had the highest leaf-to-stem ratio.

All vegetative characteristics including relative water content and rate of photosynthesis and majority of yield attributes (leaf area, leaf fresh and dry weight, root fresh weight, shoot fresh weight, total plant dry weight) showed greater values at the second harvest compared to the first.

\subsection{\textit{Mentha crispata} (Curly Mint)}

The plants are hemisperious and have 3-7 lobed leaves. The \textcite{rhs2026crispa} indicated that \textit{Mentha spicata} var. \textit{crispa} (curly spearmint) is a creeping rhizome aromatic perennial that can reach a height of 60 cm. Leaves are broad, crinkled and bright green.

Tiny pale purple flowers are in whorls at the tips of the spikes in summer. The \textcite{rhs2026crispa} lists several synonyms for this plant:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Mentha spicata} `Crispa'
    \item Cristated Spearmint.
    \item \textit{Mentha crispa} L.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Cultivation Requirements}

\textcite{rhs2026crispa} recommends Curly mint for all soil types – chalk, clay, loam or sand – provided it is moist. The soil should be moist and well drained although it will tolerate poorly drained conditions. A moisture-loving plant that should not be over wet in winter but well drained. It prefers full sun to part shade and will grow best in west, south or east facing locations.

\textcite{rhs2026crispa} says it could be a problem in terms of growth management. They recommend to lessen the root run, deep containers and placing in the soil, and small contained beds. Plants growing in pots may be divided every other year.

\section{Abiotic stress in mint cultivation with emphasis on salt stress}

Salinity is one of the most important abiotic stresses that affect plant growth and productivity particularly in controlled cultivation system where salinity can build up in the root zone of plants \parencite{Hasanuzzaman2021}. Salt stress is known to affect negatively growth, plant photosynthetic pathway, and secondary metabolites production in medicinal and aromatic plant as reported by \textcite{AcostaMotos2017}. In medicinal and spice crops it results in an osmotic and ion stress, and consequently a reduction in growth and yield; sometimes this effect is more pronounced in container-grown crops where the root space is small \parencite{Hasanuzzaman2021}.

\section{Nutrient solutions in soilless mint production.}

In soilless substrates for mint production, the substrates contain only little nutrients and the nutrient supply is provided with the irrigation water, so defined nutrient solutions are used, in which all essential plant nutrients are known to be present in a defined concentration. The most general form used is that of \textcite{Hoagland1950} which is developed from earlier work by Hoagland and Snyder (1933) and Hoagland and Arnon (1938).

The solution provides approximately 210 ppm nitrogen, 235 ppm potassium, 200 ppm calcium, 31 ppm phosphorus, 64 ppm sulphur and 49 ppm magnesium in the form of macronutrient salts (potassium nitrate, calcium nitrate, ammonium dihydrogen phosphate and magnesium sulphate). In addition to manganese chloride, zinc sulfate, copper sulfate and sodium molybdate, boric acid is added if micronutrients are necessary. Iron is fed as ferric tartrate, and is now almost always replaced by a chelated iron, such as Fe-EDTA, which is available to plants over a wider range of pH.

The original formulation was for sand and solution culture situations where the nutrient solution is regularly changed, and is diluted for use in container production using organic substrates where salts accumulate between irrigations. There are, however, a number of different solutions which are appropriate modifications of the solution, such as changing the iron chelate, the potassium:calcium ratio or the source of phosphate, and ``modified Hoagland solution'' does not refer to just one of these.

The Hoagland-type solutions are generally used in salinity research in mint. For example, \textcite{Morshedloo2025} used 25\% Hoagland solution at the initial growth stage and full strength Hoagland solution at the mature plant stage and then salinity stress treatments (0, 50 and 100~mM NaCl).

\section{Physiological responses of plants to salt stress}

\subsection{Chlorophyll content and photosynthesis}

General responses to salinity stress include reduced chlorophyll content and photosynthetic efficiency, which occurs as a result of damage to chloroplasts, and stomatal limitation \parencite{Kalaji2021}. These effects can directly result in reduced biomass in saline conditions \parencite{MunnsTester2008}.

\subsection{Water Relations and Osmotic Adjustments}

Salt stress impacts on water uptake ability and lowers the leaf water potential, which triggers osmotic adjustment such as compatible solute accumulation as described by \textcite{AcostaMotos2017}.

\section{Biochar effect on substrate properties and salinity stress}

Moreover, biochar can be used to increase the water holding capacity, C.E.C and nutrient retention in growing media, which may reduce the salinity stress effect on the plants \parencite{Akhtar2021}. Moreover, biochar can also reduce Na\textsuperscript{+} availability in the root zone that can help to reduce ion toxicity under salinity conditions \parencite{Joseph2021}.

\section{The identification of the gap in the study and the justification of the same.}

From the information in the previous sections it is clear that mint is an economically important crop, peat moss has important environmental limitations, biochar has proven useful as a sustainable alternative, and salt stress is a real threat to greenhouse crop production. However, one key knowledge gap remains: whether or not biochar can increase salt tolerance in mint. Especially, little studies have been conducted about the impact of biochar amendment in M. species under the salt stress conditions. Research is lacking that systematically compares multiple mint species to determine whether there is species specific response and/or genotype differences in benefits of biochar.

This knowledge of interactions is critical for developing grower recommendations for sustainable management of salt stress in mint production. This gap can be seen as the point that the current study has emerged.

\section{Aim and objectives of the study}

The main goal of this study is to observe the possibility of biochar as an alternative to peat and its effects on the salinity response of three mint species. The specific objectives were to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Study salt stress effects on the growth of different mint species.
    \item Evaluate the salt stress tolerance of biochar incorporated growing media.
    \item Investigate how different kinds of mints affect physiological responses,
    \item Provide understanding of salinity effects on plant growth and physiology of unamended and biochar amended growing media.
\end{itemize}
