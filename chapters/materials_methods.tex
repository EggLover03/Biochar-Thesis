\chapter{Materials and Methods}

The experimental design and salt stress treatments were developed based on established methodologies used in previous biochar and salinity studies \textcite{Akhtar2021, Hasanuzzaman2021}.

\section{Experimental site and growth conditions}

The experiment was carried out in a growth chamber in a room of the faculty building. The room temperature was 26~$^{\circ}$C and the air humidity 60--70\%. The plants were grown under a 12~h light and 12~h dark photoperiod, with full-spectrum lighting at a photosynthetically active radiation of 300~$\mu$mol~m$^{-2}$~s$^{-1}$.

\section{Plant material}

Three mint taxa were used: \textit{Mentha piperita} L. (peppermint), \textit{Mentha crispata} (curly mint) and \textit{Mentha spicata} L. `Moroccan' (Moroccan mint).

The plants were bought from a commercial supplier as rooted plantlets, with 3 – 4 plantlets planted in each pot. The supplier was Oázis Kertészet Kft. in Budapest, Hungary. The plantlets were propagated by root shoots and the shoots were 2 – 3 months old at planting. The supplier has a plant certificate that the variety is a pure variety.

\section{Experimental design}

\subsection{Growing media treatments}

Each species of mint was grown under three growing media treatments: an unamended peat control and peat amended with 15 or 30 g pot$^{-1}$ of biochar. The biochar used is a commercial product (Carbozero “Nature Carbon”, Pflanzenkohle) produced from wood biomass using a multi-stage thermochemical conversion. It is manufactured by Energo Zlatá Olešnice s.r.o. (Zlatá Olešnice, Czech Republic) and distributed by Dr.~Uhlí s.r.o. (Lučany nad Nisou, Czech Republic). The manufacturer's specification is: its carbon content is at least 50\%, moisture content is at most 5\%, ash content is at most 15\%, volatile matter content is at most 10\%, the pH value is at least 7, the specific surface area at least 150 m$^2$ g$^{-1}$, and the molar H/C and O/C ratios are at most 0.7 and 0.4, respectively. The declared upper limits for risk elements, in mg kg$^{-1}$ of dry mass, are 1 for cadmium, 1 for mercury, 10 for lead, 20 for arsenic, 50 for chromium and 20 for the sum of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. The biochar was incorporated in the peat in dry form, without soaking or pre-wetting, although the manufacturer directs that the material be soaked in water for at least 48 hours before use and suggests mixing it with substrate in a ratio of one part biochar to nine parts substrate by volume. Each growing medium (unamended peat, 15 g and 30 g of biochar) was coupled with each of the three salinity levels (0, 25 and 50 mM NaCl) for \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} ‘Moroccan’, resulting in one pot for each growing medium and salinity level. This resulted in nine pots for each species and 27 pots overall (Table~\ref{tab:media}).

Each pot contained 400 g of substrate. In the biochar treatments, the biochar replaced the same mass of peat, so a pot with 15 g of biochar contained 385 g of peat and a pot with 30 g of biochar contained 370 g of peat (3.75\% and 7.5\% biochar by mass, respectively).

On 15 September 2026 all three species were potted.

The manufacturer does not state the temperature at which the material was pyrolysed. The published specification is nevertheless consistent with thorough carbonisation: a molar H/C ratio of at most 0.7 is the threshold used by the European Biochar Certificate to distinguish biochar from incompletely charred biomass, and the low volatile matter content and the specific surface area of at least 150~m$^2$~g$^{-1}$ point in the same direction. The pyrolysis temperature is therefore not given here as a figure.

The declared limits for cadmium, mercury, lead, arsenic, chromium and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons correspond to the thresholds applied under the European Biochar Certificate, which indicates that the material was produced to that standard.

\subsection{Salt stress treatments}

Three levels of salinity were used, 25 mM and 50 mM NaCl and an unsalted control. Both solutions were made by dissolving sodium chloride in distilled water, with the concentrations made in 500 mls at each concentration. Salinity concentrations were similar to those applied in other studies involving mint: \textcite{Morshedloo2025} applied 0, 50 and 100 mM NaCl in \textit{Mentha} × \textit{gracilis} and \textcite{Ounoki2021} applied 0, 5, 25 and 50 mM NaCl to spearmint for a duration of 14 days.

For each species, one pot of each growing medium × salinity combination was set up, giving a complete factorial design with one pot per combination.

\section{Cultivation practices}

\subsection{Nutrient solution}

A modified Hoagland nutrient solution was provided to the plants based on \textcite{Hoagland1950}. The composition of the full-strength solution is given in Table~\ref{tab:hoagland}. The Fe-EDTA was used in place of the ferric tartrate specified in the original formulation and the pH of the solution was changed to about 6.5.

Each pot received 100 ml of the nutrient solution twice a week and 100 ml of distilled water twice a week. The electrical conductivity of the solution was 1.8 – 2 mS~cm$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Plant protection}

Aphid infestation was recorded during the growth period and the systemic neonicotinoid insecticide, Mospilan containing acetamiprid as active ingredients, was used for control. Thus, foliar spray of this product was applied to all plants in the experiment regardless of growing medium or salinity treatment to provide an equal level of plant protection across treatments. The spray was applied on 29 September 2026, the same day the salinity treatments were applied. Infestation was more severe on \textit{M. spicata} ‘Moroccan’ than on the other two.

Mospilan 20 SG was used at 0.1 g per 0.5 litre of water. It was applied twice: the first time directly on the aphids in mid-September and the second time two weeks later, at the end of September, to prevent a new infestation.

\section{Stress application procedure}

Salinity treatments were applied on 29th September 2026, 14 days after potting. It was also applied at the later stages when the plants had developed larger shoot growth as done by \textcite{Morshedloo2025} on salinity. The pots for each salinity treatment were filled with 100 ml of the correct NaCl solution; pots in the 0 mM treatment (including unamended peat) were not filled with NaCl solution. This salinity treatment was replicated 8 days later on 7 October 2026. One more application of the NaCl solution at the same dose was made on 13 October 2026. During the treatment period, the electrical conductivity of the substrate and of the drainage water was not measured.

The NaCl solution was thus applied three times in all, on 29 September, 7 October and 13 October 2026, at intervals of eight and six days, and the plants were measured four weeks after the first application.

\section{Measured parameters}

\subsection{Growth parameters}

Plant height and leaf length and width were measured with a digital caliper at the time of the measurements, four weeks after the first NaCl application. The leaves used for the measurements were taken from the upper third of the plant, because young leaves show the symptoms of stress first. Three leaves were measured per pot, and the same three leaves were used for the leaf length and width, the Fv/Fm ratio and the chlorophyll content.

\subsection{Physiological measurements}

The relative water content (RWC) was calculated from the fresh weight (FW), the turgid weight (TW) and the dry weight (DW) of one leaf per pot as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RWC}\,(\%) = \frac{\mathrm{FW} - \mathrm{DW}}{\mathrm{TW} - \mathrm{DW}} \times 100
  \label{eq:rwc}
\end{equation}

The turgid weight was determined after soaking the leaf in distilled water for 24 hours, and the dry weight after drying the leaf at 60~$^{\circ}$C for 8 hours. The same procedure was followed for the leaves of every pot, irrespective of growing medium or salinity treatment.

The Fv/Fm ratio was measured with a FluorPen FP 100 after 30 minutes of dark adaptation. The chlorophyll content was measured with an Apogee MC-100 chlorophyll meter and is given in $\mu$mol~m$^{-2}$.

\subsection{Laboratory analyses}

All physiological measurements were made with handheld instruments in the laboratory of the faculty, namely the Apogee MC-100 chlorophyll meter and the FluorPen FP 100. These devices were chosen because they are straightforward to use and give reliable readings on intact leaves, so that no extraction or spectrophotometric determination was required. The content of essential oils was not determined, as this type of analysis is not possible at the faculty.

\section{Statistical analysis}

Three leaves were sampled from each pot, and on each leaf three readings were taken with the handheld instruments. The three readings for a leaf were analytical replicates, used to increase the accuracy with which that leaf was characterised, and were averaged to give one value per leaf for each measured parameter. The leaves taken from a single pot are subsamples of that pot rather than independent replicates of the treatment, since they share the same substrate and the same treatment.

Each combination of growing medium and salinity was represented by one pot. The three leaf values of a pot were therefore averaged in turn, and the pot, giving one value per treatment combination, was used as the unit of analysis. The effects of the biochar rate (0, 15 and 30~g) and of the salinity level (0, 25 and 50~mM NaCl) were tested separately for each species by two-way analysis of variance without an interaction term, using the biochar $\times$ salinity interaction as the error term (4 degrees of freedom), followed by Tukey's test for pairwise comparisons. The significance level was 0.05. The calculations were made in Python (SciPy). The fresh, turgid and dry weights, measured on one leaf per pot, were not analysed statistically. Because of the lack of replication, the tests are exploratory.

Because of the lack of replication between pots, differences between treatments should be interpreted with caution.
