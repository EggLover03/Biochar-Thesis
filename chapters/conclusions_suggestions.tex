\chapter{Conclusions and Suggestions}

\section{Comparison of salt stress responses among mint species}

Salinity reduced growth in all three taxa, and the reduction increased with the NaCl concentration. In unamended peat, plant height at 50~mM NaCl was 31\% lower than at 0~mM in \textit{M. piperita}, 44\% lower in \textit{M. crispata} and 51\% lower in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', and the chlorophyll content was reduced by 29\%, 51\% and 59\%, respectively. \textit{M. piperita} was therefore the least affected and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' the most affected taxon, with \textit{M. crispata} in between. This agrees with the report of \textcite{Singh2022} that physiological differences among mint species determine their responses to environmental stress. The Fv/Fm ratio decreased only slightly (3 to 7\%) in all taxa, which suggests that the photosynthetic apparatus was only mildly damaged at the concentrations used, while the decrease in the chlorophyll content is in line with the effect of salinity described by \textcite{Kalaji2021}.

The three taxa differed not only in how much they were affected but in which organ responded first. In \textit{M. piperita} and in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' the stems were the more sensitive measure: at 50~mM in unamended peat their heights fell by 30.7 and 50.8\% while their leaf lengths fell by 21.1 and 33.4\%. In \textit{M. crispata} the relationship was reversed, leaf length falling by 54.6\% against a height reduction of 44.2\%, the largest proportional reduction of any growth parameter recorded in the experiment. A ranking of the taxa by sensitivity therefore depends on which parameter is chosen, and a grower concerned with leaf yield rather than with plant size would rank \textit{M. crispata}, not `Moroccan', as the most affected.

The taxa also differed in how early they responded. At the intermediate concentration of 25~mM, \textit{M. piperita} and \textit{M. crispata} had lost 15.2 and 16.5\% of their height, whereas \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' had already lost 29.7\%, approximately twice as much. That taxon was thus affected at a concentration the other two tolerated comparatively well, which is relevant where irrigation water is only moderately saline. For every taxon and every parameter the values at 25~mM lay between those of the control and the 50~mM treatment, so within the range tested the response was graded rather than abrupt, and no concentration behaved as a threshold beyond which growth collapsed.

\section{Role of biochar in mitigating salt stress effects}

Biochar increased plant height and leaf size at every salinity level, and the 30~g rate was more effective than the 15~g rate in most cases. Biochar also reduced the loss of growth and chlorophyll caused by salinity: between 0 and 50~mM, plant height decreased by 31\%, 23\% and 18\% in \textit{M. piperita} with 0, 15 and 30~g of biochar, and by 51\%, 36\% and 28\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The benefit of biochar under salt stress was largest in the two taxa that were most affected by salinity, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. Biochar did not significantly change the Fv/Fm ratio. The relative water content gave results that could not be interpreted, because it was much lower in the pots with biochar than in the unamended pots of \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' and did not decrease with salinity; the weights behind this result should be checked before any conclusion is drawn from it.

Two features of the biochar effect deserve emphasis. The first is that the advantage grew with the severity of the stress. Without salt, the 30~g rate produced plants between 23.3 and 23.8\% taller than those in unamended peat in all three taxa, a remarkably uniform response. At 50~mM the same comparison gave 46.2\% in \textit{M. piperita}, 60.3\% in \textit{M. crispata} and 79.5\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The taxa therefore responded to biochar almost identically when unstressed and very differently when stressed, which indicates that what biochar contributed under salinity was not the same as what it contributed in its absence.

The second is that biochar reduced the proportional loss, not merely the absolute one. Had the amendment simply made the plants larger, the percentage of height lost between 0 and 50~mM would have been about the same in every growing medium. Instead that percentage fell with each increase in the biochar rate in all three taxa, from 30.7 to 18.0\% in \textit{M. piperita}, from 44.2 to 27.7\% in \textit{M. crispata} and from 50.8 to 28.4\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The plants grown with biochar were thus less sensitive to the salt, and not only bigger at the outset. This distinction matters for interpretation, because only the second of the two explanations amounts to a mitigation of stress.

It must be repeated that these comparisons rest on one pot per combination. The consistency of the pattern across three taxa, two biochar rates and three salinity levels makes it unlikely to have arisen by chance alone, but the design does not allow that to be tested, and the magnitudes given above should be treated as indications rather than as measured effects.

\section{Comparison with previous studies}

The alleviation of salt stress by biochar found in this study is consistent with the reports that biochar can mitigate salinity stress \parencite{Farhangi2017, Akhtar2021}. These authors attribute it to better water retention, nutrient availability and microbial activity, and \textcite{Joseph2021} adds that biochar can lower the availability of sodium in the root zone. These mechanisms were not measured here, because the electrical conductivity of the substrate and the sodium content of the plants were not determined, so they cannot be confirmed. The positive response of the mint taxa to biochar also agrees with \textcite{Zheljazkov2019}, who found that biochar can influence the growth of mint. The NaCl concentrations used (25 and 50~mM) were within the range used in other studies with mint \parencite{Morshedloo2025, Ounoki2021}, but those studies used other substrates and durations, so a direct comparison of the size of the effects is not possible.

\section{Main conclusions}

The main conclusions of this study are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Salinity (25 and 50~mM NaCl) reduced plant height, leaf size and chlorophyll content in \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', and slightly reduced the Fv/Fm ratio.
  \item \textit{M. piperita} was the least and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' the most sensitive to salt stress.
  \item Peat in which 15 or 30~g of the 400~g of substrate was replaced by biochar gave taller plants and larger leaves than unamended peat, and reduced the decrease in growth and chlorophyll content caused by salinity; the 30~g rate was generally more effective.
  \item The effect of biochar was greater under salt stress and in the more sensitive taxa.
  \item Because of the limited replication, these results are preliminary.
\end{itemize}

\section{Practical implications and suggestions for indoor mint cultivation}

Within the limits of this experiment, replacing part of the peat with biochar (3.75 to 7.5\% by mass) did not harm the three mint taxa and improved their growth, especially under salt stress. Biochar may therefore be a useful partial alternative to peat in the growing media of mint grown indoors, where salts can accumulate in the small root zone of containers \parencite{Dumroese2018, Hasanuzzaman2021}. The higher rate of 30~g can be recommended in preference to 15~g, and growers using saline water should expect \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' to be more sensitive than \textit{M. piperita}. These suggestions should be confirmed with replicated trials before being applied in production.

The choice between the two rates is worth setting out more precisely, because the two did not differ equally under all conditions. Without salt the step from 15 to 30~g added a further 16.6, 16.9 and 11.5 percentage points of height in \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', so the higher rate was already worthwhile. Under salinity the gap widened: at 50~mM the 30~g rate exceeded the 15~g rate by 27.8, 28.4 and 33.5 percentage points in the same order. Where irrigation water is of good quality a grower might reasonably use the lower rate, since much of the benefit is obtained at 15~g; where the water is saline the higher rate returns considerably more, and the case for using it strengthens as the salinity rises.

The proportions used here are also convenient in practice. Replacing 15 or 30~g in 400~g of substrate corresponds to 3.75 or 7.5\% by mass, which is below the one part in nine by volume that the manufacturer of the biochar recommends for garden soil. A grower following the manufacturer's instruction would therefore be applying rather more biochar than was tested here, and the results of this experiment cannot be extended to that higher rate; what they show is that benefits are already obtained at proportions well below it.

One practical caution follows from the way the biochar was handled. The manufacturer directs that the material be soaked in water for at least 48 hours before use, and in this experiment it was incorporated dry. Dry biochar is hydrophobic and takes up water from the surrounding substrate as it wets, so the amended pots may have been drier than the unamended ones during the first days after potting. Since the plants nevertheless grew better with biochar, this is unlikely to have produced the differences reported, but a grower following the manufacturer's instruction would be expected to obtain at least the benefits seen here, and the soaking step is in any case advisable.

Finally, the species chosen matters as much as the substrate. \textit{M. piperita} retained about seven tenths of its height at the highest salinity in unamended peat, whereas \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' retained less than half. A grower working with saline water would be better served by \textit{M. piperita}, and the combination of that taxon with the higher biochar rate gave the smallest proportional loss recorded in this experiment, 18.0\%.

\section{Limitations of the study}

The principal limitation of this study is its limited replication. Each combination of growing medium and salinity was represented by a single pot within each species, and the leaves sampled from a pot are subsamples of that pot rather than independent replicates of the treatment. Differences observed between treatments may therefore partly reflect differences between individual pots, and the findings should be regarded as preliminary.

The relative water content could not be used. The values calculated for the biochar pots fall well below the range compatible with living tissue, and the measure rose rather than fell with salinity, both of which point to the turgid weights rather than to the water status of the plants. Since the soaking and weighing procedure was identical for every pot, the cause could not be identified, and this parameter is excluded from the interpretation. Any repetition should blot the leaves before weighing and check the turgid weight against the fresh weight before the figures are used.

An aphid infestation occurred during the growing period and was controlled with two sprays of Mospilan 20 SG, the first in mid-September and the second at the end of September. The insecticide was applied uniformly to every plant regardless of treatment, so plant protection itself is not confounded with the experimental treatments. Any differences in the severity of the infestation before treatment may nevertheless have affected growth independently of the growing medium and salinity applied.

The experiment was conducted over a single growing period at one location, and in a growth chamber rather than under field conditions. The results describe the behaviour of these species under the conditions tested and should not be extended to other environments, seasons or production systems without further work.

The analysis was made on the mean of three leaves per pot, and the fresh, turgid and dry weights on one leaf per pot, so the statistical tests are exploratory. The electrical conductivity and pH of the biochar and of the substrate were not measured, so it is not known how much salt the biochar itself added to the treatments. The plants were measured only once, four weeks after the first application of NaCl, and the relative water content of the pots with biochar differed from that of the unamended pots in two of the taxa in a way that could not be explained.

\section{Recommendations for future research}

Future experiments should use several pots per treatment, so that treatment effects can be separated from differences between pots and tested with a full factorial analysis of variance. The electrical conductivity and pH of the biochar and of the substrate, and the sodium content of the plants, should be measured to find out how biochar acts under salt stress. Higher biochar rates, other NaCl concentrations and a complete replacement of peat could be compared, together with measurements made at several times during the experiment. The determination of the essential oil content, which was not possible at the faculty, would show whether the improved growth is accompanied by a change in the quality of the mint. Finally, the weights used to calculate the relative water content should be measured in the same way for all treatments.
