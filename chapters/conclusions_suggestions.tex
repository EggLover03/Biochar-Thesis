\chapter{Conclusions and Suggestions}

\section{Comparison of salt stress responses among mint species}

All three taxa were inhibited by salinity and this inhibition was increased as NaCl concentration was increased. Plant height at 50~mM NaCl was 31\%, 44\% and 51\% shorter than at 0~mM in \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', respectively, while the chlorophyll content was decreased by 29\%, 51\% and 59\%, respectively, under the same conditions. \textit{M. piperita} was thus least affected and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' most affected taxon, \textit{M. crispata} being in the middle. This is consistent with the report of \textcite{Singh2022} on physiological distinctions between mint species accounting for their reactions to environmental stress. There was only slight reduction (3-7\%) in Fv/Fm in all taxa, indicating only mild damage to the photosynthetic apparatus by the concentrations used, and the decrease in the chlorophyll content was comparable to the effect of salinity given by \textcite{Kalaji2021}.

The three taxa were not only affected differently, but also when they reacted first, in what organ. Of the two measurements, the stems of \textit{M. piperita} and of \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' were more sensitive: at 50~mM, their heights were reduced by 30.7 and 50.8\% respectively, and the lengths of their leaves reduced by 21.1 and 33.4\%. The reverse was true for \textit{M. crispata}, for in which case the length of the leaves decreased by 54.6\% and the height by 44.2\%, the greatest percentage decrease recorded for any growth parameter. The sensitivity of the taxa is therefore dependent on the parameter being used: if leaf production is the most important consideration for a grower, then the most sensitive is \textit{M. crispata}, not `Moroccan'.

The taxa also varied in their response times. At the intermediate concentration of 25~mM, \textit{M. piperita} and \textit{M. crispata} had reduced their height by 15.2 and 16.5\% respectively, while \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' had already reduced by 29.7\% which was about twice that of the others. That taxon was therefore impacted at a salinity that other two were comparatively tolerant of, which may be relevant in the case of irrigation water that is only moderately saline. For each taxon and each parameter, values at 25~mM fell between those of the control and 50~mM, indicating a graded rather than threshold response to the 25~mM tested.

\section{Role of biochar in mitigating salt stress effects}

Plant height and leaf size was enhanced by biochar at all salinity levels, with 30~g being more effective than 15~g at most rates. Growth reduction and chlorophyll loss under salinity stress was also reduced by biochar: the plant height reduction of \textit{M. piperita} was 31\%, 23\% and 18\% for 0, 15 and 30~g of biochar per pot; whereas, under salinity stress, the plant height reduction of \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' was 51\%, 36\% and 28\% for 0, 15 and 30~g of biochar per pot, respectively. The positive effect of biochar was shown to be highest in the two taxa most sensitive to salinity, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The Fv/Fm ratio did not significantly change in presence of biochar. The relative water content was contradictory as it was much lower in the pots containing biochar than in the unamended pots of \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' and did not decline with salinity; the weights behind this result should be verified before any conclusions are drawn from it.

Two aspects of the biochar effect should be highlighted. The first being that the advantage increased as the stress increased. Remarkably consistent responses were obtained for the different taxa for the 30~g rate, with plants being between 23.3 and 23.8\% taller than in unamended peat. This comparison at 50~mM yielded the following results: 46.2\% in \textit{M. piperita}, 60.3\% in \textit{M. crispata} and 79.5\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The taxa thus responded very similarly to biochar under unstressed conditions and very differently under the stressed conditions, suggesting that the biochar effect under salinity was different from that under non-salinity.

The second is that biochar decreased the proportional loss, rather than just the absolute loss. If the amendment had only made the plants bigger, then the proportion of height lost from 0 to 50~mM would have been approximately the same in all growing media. That percentage, on the other hand, declined as the biochar rate increased in all the three taxa ranging from 30.7 to 18.0\% for \textit{M. piperita}, 44.2 to 27.7\% for \textit{M. crispata} and 50.8 to 28.4\% for \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The plants cultivated in the biochar were accordingly less salt-sensitive than the other ones, and not only larger from the beginning. This distinction is important for interpretation purposes, since only the second of the two is a mitigation of stress.

Once again, it should be pointed out that these comparisons are based on 1 pot per combination. The pattern is consistent across three taxa, two biochar rates and three salinity levels, and is probably not purely coincidental, but cannot be tested by the design, and the magnitudes reported above are the guide lines for the effect, not the measured effect.

\section{Comparison with previous studies}

This chapter compares the findings of the present work with those of earlier research.

In the present study, alleviation of salt stress by biochar was in line with the other reports which stated that salinity stress can be alleviated by biochar \parencite{Farhangi2017, Akhtar2021}. These authors attribute this to its improved water holding capacity, nutrient availability, and microbial activity, and \textcite{Joseph2021} include the reduction in sodium availability in the root zone. These mechanisms were not measured here, since the electrical conductivity of the substrate and the sodium content of the plants were not measured and cannot be confirmed. The positive response of the mint taxa to biochar also conforms to that reported by \textcite{Zheljazkov2019} that biochar could have a growth effect on mint. The concentrations of NaCl used (25 and 50~mM) were within the range employed in other studies conducted on mint \parencite{Morshedloo2025, Ounoki2021}. However, these other studies were conducted with different substrates and lengths of time and cannot be directly compared with the size of the effects in 2021.

This is interesting in light of the reports concerning the pattern of the benefit of biochar with the severity of stress. Biochar has been found to enhance the salt tolerance of plants, as opposed to just growth of plants as described by \textcite{Farhangi2017} and \textcite{Akhtar2021}, and the present results differentiate between these two options more directly than the mere measurement of growth. If biochar had only been used as a growth promoter the relative reduction due to the salt would have been comparable between amended and unamended pots, which is not the case as it reduces in \textit{M. piperita} by 30.7 to 18.0\%, in \textit{M. crispata} by 44.2 to 27.7\% and in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' by 50.8 to 28.4\%. The almost identical responses among the three taxa in the absence of salt and the very different response in the presence of salt again suggests the same.

The responses of the two physiological measures are also worth comparison with the literature. In this experiment, chlorophyll content was more sensitive to plant stress compared to chlorophyll fluorescence, which was reported by \textcite{Kalaji2021}. The chlorophyll content decreased by up to 58.9\% whereas the Fv/Fm ratio decreased by at most 7.4\%. This meant that the plants lost a good portion of their colour and the efficiency of the remaining photosystem was maintained. This is in accord with the first account of salt stress from \textcite{AcostaMotos2017} that the initial effects of salinity are mediated by water relations and ion balance and not through direct damage to the photosynthetic machinery; however, the measurements made here do not allow any conclusions about the mechanism to be drawn.

All of these comparisons are subject to a warning. The studies reported were replicated and, in most cases, different species, substrates, salinity and duration, whereas the present experiments were one pot per combination for four weeks in a growth chamber. Agreement in direction is therefore encouraging, but only in direction and no claim is made here that the magnitudes are comparable.

\section{Main conclusions}

The following are the main conclusions of this study:

\begin{itemize}
  \item In \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', salinity (25 and 50~mM NaCl) decreased the height of the plants, leaf size, chlorophyll content, and slightly reduced the Fv/Fm ratio.
  \item \textit{M. piperita} was the most salt tolerant and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' the most salt sensitive.
  \item Peat amended with 15 or 30~g biochar: taller plants and larger leaves than unamended peat and decreased salinity-induced reduction of growth and chlorophyll content; 30~g rate in general more effective. Biochar had more pronounced effects under salt stress and for the more sensitive taxa.
  \item Limited replication, these results are preliminary.
\end{itemize}

The position as per the objectives given in Section~2.10 is as follows. The first aim – to determine how the growth of the taxa responded to salt stress was realised – height, leaf length and leaf width were measured at each salinity level for each taxon and each of these measures decreased with increasing salinity. The second – assess the impact of biochar amended growing media on salt tolerance – was addressed in that both levels of biochar application reduced the loss proportion of salt in all the taxa, but did not establish the magnitude of the reduction with one pot per combination. The third objective was to be able to compare physiological responses within taxa: this was achieved for the chlorophyll content and Fv/Fm ratio but not for the relative water content which was not interpretable. The fourth, describing the differences in response to salinity among growing media, was addressed descriptively by the proportional losses. reported in Section~4.4.

It is important to note that two questions were not addressed in the study. It was not determined how the biochar was reducing the effect of salt; the electrical conductivity of the substrate and the sodium levels in the plants were not measured, meaning it was not known if it was because of water retention, nutrient supply, binding of sodium, or a combination of these. It is also not possible to say if the differences in response to biochar are consistent between the taxa; each is based on a single pot. Each of these questions can be answered by a replicated experiment, similar to the one described in Section~5.7.

\section{Practical implications and suggestions for indoor mint cultivation}

The biochar application in this experiment did not negatively affect the three mint taxa, but did promote growth under salt stress (3.75 – 7.5\% biochar by mass). Biochar could therefore be a potentially useful partial substitute for peat in the growing media of mint cultivated indoors where salts can build up in the limited root space of containers \parencite{Dumroese2018, Hasanuzzaman2021}. For the higher rate (30~g) this is recommended as being more effective than 15~g and growers with saline water should be aware that \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' may be more sensitive than \textit{M. piperita}. Replication experiments should be performed to verify these suggestions before putting them into production.

There was some disparity in the two rates which it is necessary to specify more accurately before deciding which of the two rates it is better to use. The additional 16.6, 16.9 and 11.5 percentage points of height achieved by the 30~g rate in \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' respectively, made the higher rate justified even without salt. Following the same pattern, at 50~mM the difference became wider, the 30~g rate being 27.8, 28.4 and 33.5 percentage points higher than the 15~g rate, respectively. Where irrigation water is of good quality a grower may consider using the lower rate, as much of the benefit will be gained at 15~g; and the more saline the water is, the more benefit will be gained at the higher rate, and the greater the justification for its use.

Here are also proportions which are more handy in practice. The amount of replacement needed for 15~g or 30~g in 400~g of substrate is 3.75\% or 7.5\% by mass, which is less than the 1 in 9 part by volume that the manufacturer of the biochar recommends for garden soil. Thus, a grower following the manufacturer's instructions will use somewhat more biochar than was tested here and the conclusions from this experiment may not be applied to this higher rate, but only indicate that benefits are already being realized at lower rates.

One practical warning from the handling of the biochar. The manufacturer recommends that the material be soaked in water for a minimum of 48 hours prior to use; it was used in this experiment as a dry material. The amended pots could have been drier than the unamended pots in the initial days after potting due to the hydrophobic properties of the dry biochar that absorbs water from the surrounding substrate when it is wet. The plants did still grow better with biochar however, so this is not likely to have caused the differences reported and in any event a grower following the manufacturers' instructions would be expected to gain at least the benefits of this reported, and the soaking step is always recommended.

Last but not least, the species selected is as important as the substrate itself. \textit{M. piperita} kept approximately seven tenths of its height at the highest salinity in unamended peat, while \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' lost more than half its height. The taxon \textit{M. piperita} would be better suited to a grower using saline water and in this experiment combined with the higher biochar rate returned the smallest proportional loss (18.0\%).

\section{Limitations of the study}

The study has its own limitations.

The small number of replications is the main restriction of this study. The pots of each combination of growing medium and salinity were not replicated within each species, and the leaves collected from a pot were not a replicate of the pot, but rather a sub-sample of the pot. The variation noted between the treatments might partly be due to variation between pots and the results should be considered preliminary. A relative water content was not available.

The values calculated for biochar pots are much lower than the values compatible with living tissue and the measurement increase rather than decreases with salinity, both of which indicate the turgid weights, not the water status of the plants. This parameter was not included in the interpretation because the cause could not be determined as the soaking and weighing procedure was the same for all pots. The weight of any repetition should be the turgid weight and should be compared with fresh weight before the rep is used.

Aphid was present during the growing season and was managed with two applications of Mospilan 20 SG – mid-September and end of September. All the plants were treated with the same insecticide regardless of treatment, thus plant protection itself is not confounded with the experimental treatments. The differences, if any, in the degree of infestation prior to treatment treatment may, however, have had a significant effect on growth, separate from the growing medium and salinity used.

The experiment was carried out with one crop at one site and not in the field. These results are for the conditions tested and are not necessarily extrapolatable to other environments, seasons or production systems without additional research.

The analysis performed was exploratory because it was based on the mean value of three leaves/pot and the fresh, turgid and dry weight values were taken from one leaf/pot. There was no measurement for electrical conductivity or pH of the biochar or the substrate, so it is unknown how much salt the biochar contributed to the treatments. The relative water content of biochar pots was measured only once, four weeks after the first application of NaCl, and was different than the unamended pots in two of the taxa, for which no explanation could be provided.

\section{Recommendations for future research}

In future experiments several pots should be used per treatment to separate treatment effects from pot effects and to test the treatment effects with a full factorial analysis of variance. Electrical conductivity and pH of both the biochar and of the substrate should be measured to determine the effects of biochar under salt stress, as well as the sodium content of the plants. Other concentrations of NaCl as well as full peat replacement might be compared to higher biochar rates, and measurements taken at various points throughout the experiment. The essential oil content was not determined at the faculty, but if this was carried out, it would demonstrate whether or not the enhanced growth would be linked to a quality change in the mint. Last but not at all least the weights used in determining the relative water content for any of the treatments should be taken in the same way.

Replication is necessary because of the design used here. There are three taxa, three growing media and three salinity levels, for a total of twenty-seven combinations and one pot for each combination. If this is not feasible, the better option would be to decrease factor levels instead of replications in a more informative course. If the intermediate salinity is dropped, it would be possible to use three pots for each of eighteen combinations; if the biochar rate is tested, it would be possible to use three pots for each of eighteen combinations of biochar rate and taxon. Either of these would allow for testing the effects in a statistical manner, which the present design does not, and would be better than covering each of these combinations with one pot.

The measurements would also be helpful if they were repeated throughout the experiment instead of only once at the conclusion of the experiment. The results reported here all refer to one time point four weeks after the initial application of salt, and it is unclear whether the amended and unamended plants separated over time or whether both plants became similar until some threshold was reached. That can be answered by measuring the height of the plant each week, without any equipment except a ruler, at little expense, and it would indicate when the biochar started to have an effect.

Two additional observations from this experiment can pose specific questions. The first is for taxa: in the absence of salt, the benefits of biochar were almost the same in all three, but in the presence of salt, the benefits of biochar were significantly different, which implies that the differences between taxa may have more to do with their salt tolerance than their use of the amendment. This could be resolved by repeating the experiment with more taxa. The second relates to the biochar itself, which wasn't soaked but was added dry, but the manufacturer recommends at least 48 hours' soaking. A comparison at the same rate would have been of direct practical interest to growers, and this experiment, which has only been conducted with dry biochar, would have answered the question of whether the preparation of biochar affects the benefit achieved from it.
