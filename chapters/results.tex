\chapter{Results}

The values given in this chapter are the means of three different leaves sampled from each pot, except for the fresh, turgid and dry weights, which were measured on one leaf per pot. Each combination of species, growing medium and salinity level is represented by a single pot, so the differences between treatments are described but were not tested statistically. Percentage differences are calculated from the means in Tables~\ref{tab:growth} and~\ref{tab:physiology}.

\section{Growth responses of mint species to salt stress}

Salinity reduced the growth of all three taxa (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). In unamended peat, plant height at 50~mM NaCl was 31\% lower than at 0~mM in \textit{M. piperita}, 44\% lower in \textit{M. crispata} and 51\% lower in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. Leaf length and leaf width followed the same pattern: in unamended peat, leaf length at 50~mM was 21\%, 55\% and 33\% lower than at 0~mM, and leaf width 19\%, 44\% and 24\% lower, in the same order of taxa. The reduction was already visible at 25~mM, where the values were in most cases intermediate between those of the 0 and 50~mM treatments.

\textit{M. piperita} was the tallest taxon (36.1 to 64.4~cm), followed by \textit{M. crispata} (24.0 to 53.2~cm), while \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' was clearly the shortest (9.1 to 22.8~cm).

\section{Effects of growing media on plant growth under salt stress}

Plants grown with biochar were taller and had larger leaves than plants in unamended peat at every salinity level (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). Without salt (0~mM), plant height was 7\%, 7\% and 12\% higher with 15~g of biochar, and 24\%, 24\% and 23\% higher with 30~g, than in unamended peat for \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', respectively. The difference between the biochar rates and unamended peat was larger under salt stress: at 50~mM, plant height with 30~g of biochar was 46\%, 60\% and 79\% higher than in unamended peat for the three taxa, respectively, and with 15~g it was 18\%, 32\% and 46\% higher.

Biochar also reduced the loss of growth caused by salinity. The decrease in plant height between 0 and 50~mM was 31\%, 23\% and 18\% in \textit{M. piperita} with 0, 15 and 30~g of biochar, respectively; 44\%, 31\% and 28\% in \textit{M. crispata}; and 51\%, 36\% and 28\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The same trend was found for leaf length and width, with two exceptions: the decrease in leaf length of \textit{M. piperita} was larger with 15~g of biochar (24\%) than in unamended peat (21\%), and the decrease in leaf width of \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' was larger with 30~g (20\%) than with 15~g (16\%), although still smaller than in unamended peat (24\%). For example, in \textit{M. crispata} the leaf length at 50~mM was 55\% lower than at 0~mM in unamended peat, but only 25\% and 17\% lower with 15 and 30~g of biochar.

\section{Physiological responses of mint species to salt stress}

The Fv/Fm ratio ranged from 0.787 to 0.865 (Table~\ref{tab:physiology}). It was slightly lower at 50~mM than at 0~mM in all taxa and growing media, by 3 to 7\%, and the biochar rate changed it by at most 4\% at a given salinity level.

The chlorophyll content decreased more strongly with salinity. In unamended peat, the chlorophyll content at 50~mM was 29\% lower than at 0~mM in \textit{M. piperita}, 51\% lower in \textit{M. crispata} and 59\% lower in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. With 30~g of biochar the decreases were 11\%, 21\% and 28\%, respectively. At 50~mM, the chlorophyll content with 30~g of biochar was 33\%, 83\% and 109\% higher than in unamended peat for the three taxa, respectively. One exception to the general trend was \textit{M. crispata} in unamended peat, where the chlorophyll content at 25~mM (30.05) was higher than at 0~mM (21.67).

The relative water content (RWC) ranged from 69.5 to 99.1\% (Table~\ref{tab:physiology}). In \textit{M. piperita} it was 10\%, 9\% and 7\% lower at 50~mM than at 0~mM with 0, 15 and 30~g of biochar, respectively, and in \textit{M. crispata} 3\%, 6\% and 4\% lower. In \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' the RWC was 9\% and 5\% lower at 50~mM with 15 and 30~g of biochar, whereas in unamended peat it was higher at 50~mM (99.1\%) than at 0~mM (85.3\%). At 50~mM, the RWC of \textit{M. piperita} with 15 and 30~g of biochar was 7\% and 11\% higher than in unamended peat; in \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' it was not higher with biochar. The fresh, turgid and dry weights of the sampled leaf are given in Table~\ref{tab:weights}.

\section{Interaction between mint species, growing media, and salt stress}

The effect of salinity depended on the growing medium and on the taxon. In all three taxa the reductions in plant height and chlorophyll content caused by 50~mM NaCl were smaller with biochar than in unamended peat, and the reduction became smaller with the higher biochar rate; leaf size mostly followed the same pattern. The relative benefit of biochar under salt stress was largest in the two taxa that were the most affected by salinity in unamended peat, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', and smallest in \textit{M. piperita}. In contrast, the Fv/Fm ratio was only slightly affected by either factor.
