\chapter{Results}

All values stated in this chapter are the mean values of three different leaves taken from each pot, except for fresh, turgid and dry weights, which were collected on one leaf per pot.

Each combination of species, growing medium and salinity level is represented by one pot, and hence the differences are described and the effects of biochar and salinity were tested on the pot means (Section~\ref{sec:anova}). The Percentage Differences are computed based on means given in Tables~\ref{tab:growth} and~\ref{tab:physiology}.

\section{Growth responses of mint species to salt stress}

All three taxa were shown to be inhibited by salinity (Table~\ref{tab:growth}). In the unamended peat, plant height after 50~mM NaCl treatment was decreased by 31\% in \textit{M. piperita}, 44\% in \textit{M. crispata} and 51\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' compared to the 0~mM NaCl treatment. The same trend was seen with leaf length and leaf width, with leaf length at 50~mM being 21\%, 55\% and 33\% less than at 0~mM, and leaf width 19\%, 44\% and 24\% less in the same order of taxa. This reduction was evident at 25~mM where in most cases the values were between the 0 and 50~mM treatments.

\textit{M. piperita} was the tallest taxon (36.1 – 64.4~cm) with \textit{M. crispata} (24.0 – 53.2~cm) and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' (9.1 – 22.8~cm) both coming next.

\subsection{\textit{Mentha piperita}}

In unamended peat, \textit{M. piperita} reached 52.11~cm, 44.17~cm with 25~mM and 36.12~cm with 50~mM salt, which equates to a loss of 15.2 and 30.7\% respectively. The difference in salinities was thus greater than the difference between the control and 25~mM, indicating that the lower salinity was more or less tolerated, as a comparative level. Leaf dimensions were less affected than height: leaf length fell from 5.77 to 5.46~cm at 25~mM and to 4.55~cm at 50~mM, losses of 5.4 and 21.1\%, and leaf width from 3.24 to 3.14 and 2.62~cm, losses of 3.1 and 19.1\%. The leaves of this taxon were practically unaffected at 25~mM but were reduced in height by approx. 1/6 suggesting that stem extension was greater affected by the salt than leaf expansion.

\subsection{\textit{Mentha crispata}}

\textit{M. crispata} was more severely impacted. Height in unamended peat fell from 43.00~cm to 35.90~cm at 25~mM and to 24.00~cm at 50~mM, losses of 16.5 and 44.2\%. In contrast to \textit{M. piperita}, the leaves of this taxon reacted more strongly than the stems, reducing leaf length from 4.65 to 3.89~cm and 2.11~cm (54.6\% reduction at the highest salinity), and leaf width from 2.50 to 2.20~cm and 1.40~cm (44.0\% reduction at the highest salinity). Of all growth parameters in the experiment, leaf length of this taxon had the greatest proportional decrease. Thus the reduction in total leaf area would have been even higher than the reduction in height would suggest, for the plants at 50~mM in unamended peat were not only shorter but also had much smaller leaves.

\subsection{\textit{Mentha spicata} `Moroccan'}

The smallest taxon throughout was \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' and it was also the most sensitive to height. It decreased by 29.7\%, approximately double the proportional decrease of the other two taxa at 25~mM, and by 50.8\% at 50~mM in unamended peat. This taxon was the only one to suffer almost a third of its height at intermediate salinity. Its leaves were less impacted with a 12.8 and 33.4\% reduction in leaf length at 25 and 50~mM respectively and a 2.9 and 23.8\% reduction in leaf width at 25 and 50~mM respectively. The pattern of this taxon is thus similar to that of \textit{M. piperita}, but in this taxon the response in height is considerably greater than the response in leaf size.

\subsection{Comparison between the taxa}

In unamended peat, \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' (50.8\%), \textit{M. crispata} (44.2\%) and \textit{M. piperita} (30.7\%) are ranked by their proportional loss of height at 50~mM, with the tallest and least affected species being \textit{M. piperita}. The order of ranking by leaf length is various with \textit{M. crispata} being the lowest (54.6\%), `Moroccan' (33.4\%) and \textit{M. piperita} (21.1\%). In \textit{M. piperita} and `Moroccan', however, the taxa were not distinguished only in terms of their salt tolerance, but rather by the first organ to be affected: height was the more sensitive criterion in the former taxa, and leaves at least as strongly affected as stems in the latter. For all three taxa all values at 25~mM were within the range of the control and 50~mM treatments, so there was no threshold response within this range of the measured parameters.

\section{Effects of growing media on plant growth under salt stress}

The 3.75\% and 7.5\% biochar rates refer to 15~g and 30~g of biochar added to each of the 400~g of substrate. Taller plants (Table~\ref{tab:growth}) and larger leaves had plants produced in biochar compared to plants in unamended peat at all salinity levels. In unamended peat, \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' height was increased by 7\%, 7\% and 12\% respectively with the addition of 15~g of biochar and by 24\%, 24\% and 23\% with 30~g of biochar. The difference between biochar rates and unamended peat showed greater under salt stress: plant height with 30~g of biochar was 46\%, 60\% and 79\% higher than in unamended peat for the three taxa, respectively, and was 18\%, 32\% and 46\% higher with 15~g of biochar. Biochar also minimized salinity-induced growth loss.

The decrease in plant height between 0 and 50~mM was 31\%, 23\% and 18\% in \textit{M. piperita} with 0, 15 and 30~g of biochar, respectively; 44\%, 31\% and 28\% in \textit{M. crispata}; and 51\%, 36\% and 28\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The same trend was observed for leaf length and width, but for \textit{M. piperita}, the leaf length reduction was greater for 15~g of biochar (24\%) than for unamended peat (21\%) and for \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', the leaf width reduction with 30~g of biochar (20\%) was greater than with 15~g of biochar (16\%), but was still lower than unamended peat (24\%). In \textit{M. crispata}, for instance, the leaves grown in 0~mM peat were 55\% longer than those grown in 50~mM peat, whereas leaves grew 25\% and 17\% longer at 15 and 30~g of biochar per peat.

\subsection{Dependence of the biochar effect on salinity}

Biochar was not found to have a consistent effect throughout the salinity levels and it became more effective as the salinity level increased. This is apparent when the height of the amended plants is expressed as a percentage of the height of the unamended plants at the same salinity. The 30~g rate resulted in plants that were, respectively, 23.6\%, 23.8\% and 23.3\% taller than the untreated control in \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. In these three taxa, the increase in plant height was approximately the same. At 25~mM the same comparison gave advantages of 31.6, 29.9 and 55.8\%, and at 50~mM of 46.2, 60.3 and 79.5\%.

This was the same at the lower level with the 15~g rate. This is beneficial when compared to unamended peat, which increased from 7.0\%, 6.9\% and 11.8\% without salt to 18.4\%, 31.9\% and 45.9\% at 50~mM, respectively for \textit{M. piperita}, \textit{M. crispata}, \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. Overall, in each taxon and rate of salinity, the difference in salt tolerance between the amended and unamended plants increased with increasing salinity.

From this it follows two things. First, in the non-saline treatments the three taxa had nearly the same responses to the increased biochar rate with differences of at most half a percent, whereas under salt the responses were very different. These variations in response to biochar, then, only became apparent under stress. Secondly, the greatest advantage, 79.5\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' at 50~mM, was obtained in the taxon that experienced the greatest disadvantage under unamended peat conditions; amended plants of that taxon at 50~mM reached 16.33~cm compared to 9.10~cm under unamended peat conditions, meaning that biochar restored a significant fraction of the growth lost due to salinity.

\section{Physiological responses of mint species to salt stress}

The Fv/Fm ratio ranged from 0.787 to 0.865 (Table~\ref{tab:physiology}). It was 3–7\% lower at 50~mM than at 0~mM for all taxa and growing media, and the biochar rate only varied it by at most 4\% at a specific salinity level.

It would seem appropriate to comment on the size of this change. In unamended peat the ratio fell from 0.863 to 0.820 in \textit{M. piperita}, from 0.841 to 0.815 in \textit{M. crispata} and from 0.850 to 0.787 in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan', reductions of 5.0, 3.1 and 7.4\%. The photosystem was largely intact in the highest salinity (0.78 - 0.86) as it only differed slightly from the range of values typically reported for unstressed leaves, whereas plant height across the same salinity range decreased 30-51\%. This meant that the growth was more inhibited than the efficiency of PSII, suggesting that the loss of growth was not due to degradation of the photosynthetic apparatus. In the unamended peat, the lowest value for the whole experiment, 0.787, was found in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' and in this case the two measurements are in agreement in showing this plant to have suffered the greatest reduction in growth in height. That treatment was the most severe.

Salinity caused more significant decrease in chlorophyll content. The chlorophyll at 50~mM decreased by 29\% from 0~mM in \textit{M. piperita}, 51\% in \textit{M. crispata} and 59\% in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' when grown in unamended peat. The reductions were 11\%, 21\% and 28\%, respectively for 30g of biochar. The intermediate rate, which resulted in intermediate amounts, was 18.3, 22.8 and 39.5\% decreases in the same order in chlorophyll, and this correlated with the corresponding decreases in plant height. The chlorophyll content at 50~mM was 33\%, 83\% and 109\% greater with 30~g of biochar compared to unamended peat for the three taxa, respectively. \textit{M. crispata} grown in the peat (unamended) was the only species that had a higher chlorophyll content at 25~mM (30.05) than at 0~mM (21.67). This one number is not consistent with the rest of the series in the table: in the 8 The chlorophyll content decreased with each increase in salinity for other combinations of taxon and growing medium and for the amended pots was reduced from 22.00 to 20.60 and 16.99 for 15~g, and from 24.85 to 23.56 and 19.57 for 30~g. The unamended series is also the series which gave the lowest chlorophyll content of the whole experiment when measured at 50~mM, and rises and falls more steeply than any other series. This value cannot be decided from one pot and is probably a result of an error in one reading; the analysis of variance of the chlorophyll content of this taxon (reported in Section~\ref{sec:anova}) gave no significant effect from either factor.

The two measures of physiology performed in tandem reacted differently to each other. The chlorophyll content was correlated closely with plant height; the reduction in chlorophyll at 50~mM salinity was 29.4\%, 50.6\% and 58.9\% for the three taxa which is close to the plant height reduction of 30.7\%, 44.2\% and 50.8\% respectively. The Fv/Fm ratio on the other hand, changed very little in all the treatments. The amount of pigment in the leaves was thus significantly depleted, but the efficiency of the remainder of photosystem II was well retained. The relative water content, as calculated from fresh, turgid and dry weights (Equation~\ref{eq:rwc}), varied from 12.4 to 99.9\% (Table~\ref{tab:physiology}).

In all pots treated with biochar, this was much lower than in the pots treated with \textit{M. crispata} (73.2 to 83.8\%) and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' (85.5 to 99.9\%) or \textit{M. piperita} (20.0 to 27.7\%) unamended pots. The RWC did not decrease with salinity; it was higher at 50~mM than at 0~mM in eight of the nine combinations of taxon and growing medium, for instance, 38\% higher in \textit{M. piperita} (unamended) and 48\% \& 46\% higher in 15 \& 30~g biochar amended \textit{M. piperita}; the only decrease occurred in \textit{M. crispata} (unamended) (5\%). The fresh, turgid and dry weight of the sample leaf is shown in Table~\ref{tab:weights}.

The values are biologically nonrealistic and hence are not interpreted here. The relative water contents in the biochar pots of 12-29\% was recorded during the time leaves were taken from the plants, and the plants in the biochar pots were turgid and alive, so these figures do not reflect true relative water contents. Another aspect of the response is also in the opposite direction to that expected, with eight of the nine combinations showing an increase in relative water content with increasing salinity. Leaves which absorb water in excess of full turgor or which retain surface water when weighing will give the same effect, four to seven times the fresh weight in the denominator of Equation~\ref{eq:rwc} will lower the calculated value. The difference was not due to the soaking or weighing as this was the same for all pots.

These differences in method between treatments and why they have occurred are not known. The relative water content is given for completeness only, but is not used to interpret the results.

The response to salinity was different in the various growing media and for the various taxa. The interaction was most pronounced when the absolute loss of plant height due to the highest salinity (50~mM) was expressed as a percentage of the plant at 0~mM in the same growing medium, thus the greatest difference was between the plants at 0 and 50~mM as a percentage of the plant at 0~mM. The proportional losses are listed in Table~\ref{tab:decline} and illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:decline}.

The pattern holds true for the three taxa. In \textit{M. piperita}, salinity of 50~mM reduced height by 30.7\% for unamended peat, 23.3\% for biochar amended peat with 15~g biochar and 18.0\% for biochar amended peat with 30~g biochar (which was approximately four tenths smaller than the unamended peat). In \textit{M. crispata} the corresponding losses were 44.2\%, 31.2\% and 27.7\%, and in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' they were 50.8\%, 35.8\% and 28.4\%. The proportional loss decreased with increasing biochar rate in each taxon and the 30~g rate resulted in the lowest proportional loss in each taxon.

The size of the benefit varied among the taxa. The proportional loss from unamended peat to the biochar amended peat was 12.7 percentage points for \textit{M. piperita}, 16.5 for \textit{M. crispata} and 22.4 for \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan'. The most salt stressed taxa in unamended peat were the most salt benefited by biochar and the taxa losing over 50\% height in unamended peat lost only just over 25\% height at the higher biochar rate.

One additional aspect of the interaction was that biochar did not simply raise growth by a fixed increment, but rather shifted growth up in a variable manner. If the proportional loss had occurred, it would have been the same in all three growing media. The loss, however, decreased with increasing the biochar rate, suggesting that the plants cultivated by using biochar are not necessarily bigger, but rather less sensitive to the salt. In chlorophyll content and (less consistently) in leaf length and width, the same was found, while the Fv/Fm ratio was only slightly influenced by either and did not exhibit a similar pattern.

Note that these proportional losses are descriptive and only based on individual pots. They are reported because they more directly express the relationship between the two factors rather than the difference between them, not because the difference between the two factors has been established as a statistically reliable difference.

\section{Statistical analysis of the effects of biochar and salinity}\label{sec:anova}

Two way analysis of variance on the pot means were used to test the effects of the biochar rates and the salinity level within each species (Table~\ref{tab:anova}). As there was one pot per combination of treatment, the interaction between biochar and salinity could not be tested separately and these tests were exploratory.

Both of these influenced significantly plant height in all three taxa ($p < 0.01$). Tukey's test showed that plant height was significantly affected by all three salinity levels for all taxa and by all biochar rates (except for 15 and 30~g in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' with a $p = 0.066$). Salinity significantly decreased the Fv/Fm ratio for all taxa ($p < 0.05$), whereas the biochar rate had a non-significant effect on the Fv/Fm ratio ($p \geq 0.10$). Leaf width was significantly affected by both factors in \textit{M. piperita} and \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' and leaf length in \textit{M. spicata} `Moroccan' ($p < 0.001$ for both) but not in \textit{M. crispata} ($p = 0.759$ and $p = 0.144$); in leaf, Salinity ($p = 0.032$), but not biochar ($p = 0.052$), significantly affected length. The relative water content was excluded from the analysis of variance, because of the above.
